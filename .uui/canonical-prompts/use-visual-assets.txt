# Use Images & Visual Assets

This skill exists because your default behavior is to produce layouts made of colored rectangles, icons, and text — visually flat, monotone, and lifeless. Real designs use IMAGES. Photography, illustrations, background textures, gradients, patterns, and visual depth. This skill teaches you when, where, and how to use them.

Every time you create a design, ACTIVELY ask yourself: "Where should there be an image here instead of an empty box?" If you can't identify at least one image opportunity in a full-page design, you're doing it wrong.

---

## Step 1: Identify Every Image Opportunity

Before finalizing any layout, scan every section and ask these questions:

### Hero / Header sections
- Does this hero have a background image, illustration, or product screenshot? It almost always should. A hero with just a colored background and text is a missed opportunity — heroes are the FIRST thing users see.
- Options: Full-bleed background photo with text overlay. Split layout with image on one side. Product screenshot/mockup floating over a gradient. Abstract illustration as background.

### Feature / Content sections
- Are features shown as icon + text only? Consider: could one or more features show an actual screenshot, photo, or illustration of the feature in action?
- The pattern "alternating image + text" (image left / text right, then flipped) is one of the most effective content patterns on the web. Use it.
- Even if icons are appropriate for a feature grid, consider adding a LARGE image above or below the grid showing the product/concept in context.

### Testimonial / Social proof sections
- Do testimonial cards have real avatar photos? They must. A testimonial without a face is dramatically less credible.
- Consider adding company logos alongside testimonials.
- Background: subtle texture or pattern behind testimonial sections adds depth.

### Team sections
- Team members MUST have photos. A team grid without photos is just a list of names. The whole point of a team section is human connection.

### Blog / Content cards
- Every blog card should have a thumbnail image. Cards without images look like unfinished wireframes.
- The image should be the first visual element in the card (top for vertical cards, left for horizontal cards).

### About / Story sections
- These almost always benefit from a large, atmospheric photo — office space, team at work, the product in context, the founder's story.

### CTA sections
- A CTA with just text and a button on a flat background is weak. Consider: background image with overlay, illustration, product mockup, or pattern.

### Backgrounds in general
- Full-bleed colored sections look better with subtle texture, noise grain, or a very soft gradient rather than flat solid color.
- Alternating between white and off-white sections is fine but BORING. Consider using an image-backed section to break the rhythm every 3-4 sections.

### Empty states / Illustrations
- 404 pages, empty states, loading screens — these are prime opportunities for illustrations or playful images.

## Step 2: Choose the Right Image Type

Not all images are created equal. Match the image type to the context:

### Photography
**When:** Hero backgrounds, team photos, testimonial avatars, about sections, lifestyle/context shots, blog thumbnails.
**Mood matching:** The photography style must match the design register. A luxury brand needs high-end studio photography. A SaaS product needs clean, bright, professional shots. A creative agency needs edgy, artistic photography. Don't use generic stock photography that could belong to any brand.
**Generate vs reference:** When creating designs, generate images that match the brand's specific aesthetic. Describe the image with detail — lighting, composition, subject, mood — not just "a photo of people working."

### Product screenshots / Mockups
**When:** Feature sections, hero areas for SaaS/apps, pricing sections (show what you get), case studies.
**Treatment:** Screenshots should be placed in device mockups (laptop, phone, browser frame) or shown with a subtle shadow/elevation. Never paste a raw screenshot flat — it needs framing context. UUI includes screen mockup components — use them.

### Illustrations
**When:** Empty states, 404 pages, onboarding flows, feature explanations of abstract concepts, playful brands. UUI has illustration components for empty states — use them.
**Style consistency:** All illustrations in a single design must share the same style. Don't mix line illustrations with 3D renders with flat vector art.

### Icons as visual elements (not just UI icons)
**When:** Feature grids, metric cards, process steps. UUI has featured icon components with colored backgrounds — use them to add visual weight to otherwise text-heavy sections.
**Sizing:** Feature icons should be substantial (48-64px), not tiny. The icon IS the visual element in a feature card — treat it with prominence.

### Background patterns & textures
**When:** Section backgrounds that need depth without being distracting. UUI has background pattern components — use them.
**Opacity:** Background patterns should be subtle — 5-15% opacity over the section background. They add texture without competing with content.
**Types:** Dot grids, line patterns, geometric patterns, gradient meshes, noise grain. UUI provides several pattern components.

### Avatars
**When:** Testimonials, team sections, activity feeds, comments, messaging, user profiles.
**Always use photos for avatars in marketing/public-facing designs.** Initials-only avatars are for app UIs where photos aren't available, not for designed marketing pages. UUI has avatar components — use them with photo fills.

### Logos / Brand marks
**When:** Social proof sections ("trusted by"), partner sections, press mentions, integration showcases.
**Treatment:** Logos should be shown in a consistent style — either all monochrome/grayscale or all in brand colors. Mixed treatment looks chaotic.

## Step 3: Image Composition & Treatment

### Aspect ratios matter
- Hero images: 16:9 or wider for full-bleed, 3:2 or 4:3 for split layouts
- Blog/card thumbnails: 16:9 or 3:2 — consistent across all cards in a grid
- Team photos: 1:1 (square) or 3:4 (portrait) — consistent across the whole grid
- Product screenshots: Natural device ratio (16:10 for laptops, 9:19.5 for phones)
- ALL images in a grid MUST share the same aspect ratio. Mixed ratios in a card grid look broken.

### Scale modes
- **FILL** (default for most cases): Image fills the container, crops edges as needed. Use for hero backgrounds, card thumbnails, avatars — anywhere the image should cover the entire area.
- **FIT**: Image fits entirely within the container, may leave gaps. Use when the entire image must be visible (logos, product screenshots where cropping would lose information).
- **TILE**: Image repeats. Use only for background patterns.
- **CROP**: Manually positioned fill. Use when you need specific control over which part of an image is visible.

### Text over images — readability is non-negotiable
When placing text over an image, the text MUST be readable. Strategies:

1. **Dark overlay**: Place a semi-transparent dark layer (40-70% opacity black/dark gray) between the image and text. Use for hero sections with white text.
2. **Gradient fade**: A gradient from solid color (bottom) to transparent (top) — text sits in the solid area. More elegant than a flat overlay. Useful for card images with text at the bottom.
3. **Scrim / vignette**: Darkening only the area around the text, not the whole image. Preserves more of the image.
4. **Text shadow**: Subtle shadow on text for light readability boost. Use sparingly — works for headings, not body text.
5. **Contained text**: Place text in a solid or frosted container that sits on top of the image. The container provides readability, the image provides atmosphere.
6. **Choose dark/muted images**: Select or generate images that are naturally dark enough for white text without additional overlay.

**The rule:** If you can't read the text clearly over the image from arm's length, the treatment is wrong. Fix it before moving on.

### Image corners and clipping
- Images inside cards should clip to the card's border radius. If the card has radius-xl (12px), the image should respect that — either by being inside a clipping frame or by having matching corner radius.
- Full-bleed hero images should have no radius (the section is edge-to-edge).
- Avatar images are always circular (radius-full) or rounded-square depending on the design system — follow UUI's avatar component.

### Image borders and containment
- Product screenshots and mockups often benefit from a subtle border (1px, border-secondary) to separate them from the background, especially when the screenshot has a white background matching the page.
- Photos generally don't need borders — the image content provides its own edge.

## Step 4: Background Treatments That Add Depth

The biggest reason AI designs look flat is that every section has a solid-color background. Fix this:

### Gradient backgrounds
- A subtle gradient (e.g., bg-primary to bg-secondary, vertically) adds dimensionality without being distracting.
- Radial gradients behind key content areas create a spotlight effect that draws attention.
- Don't overuse — one or two gradient sections per page is enough.

### Image backgrounds with overlay
- A large atmospheric photo with a 60-80% opacity color overlay creates a rich, branded section background while keeping text readable.
- This works especially well for CTA sections, testimonial sections, and closing sections.
- In dark mode, use a darker overlay opacity.

### Background patterns from UUI
- UUI has background pattern components (dot patterns, line patterns, decorative patterns). These are designed to be placed behind content at low opacity.
- Layer them: background color + pattern at 5-10% + content on top.
- They're especially effective on hero sections and CTA sections.

### Background blurs / glassmorphism
- Frosted glass effects (background blur + semi-transparent fill) create depth for floating elements like navigation bars, modals, and cards.
- Use sparingly — one or two glassmorphic elements per design, not everywhere.
- Requires a visually interesting background behind the blurred element to be effective.

### Noise / grain texture
- Adding subtle noise (1-3% opacity) to solid backgrounds gives them a tactile quality that flat color lacks.
- This is a micro-detail but it dramatically reduces the "digital flatness" of solid-color sections.

## Step 5: Visual Rhythm with Images

Images don't just fill space — they create RHYTHM in the page layout:

### The cadence rule
In a long-scroll page, users should encounter a significant visual element (image, screenshot, illustration) every 2-3 sections maximum. If there are 4+ consecutive sections that are nothing but text, icons, and colored boxes, the page has a visual desert — add an image.

### Alternating visual weight
- Heavy (image-dominant) sections should alternate with light (text-dominant) sections.
- Two image-heavy sections back-to-back feel cluttered. Two text-heavy sections back-to-back feel barren.
- Pattern: Hero (heavy) → Features grid (light) → Screenshot showcase (heavy) → Testimonials (light) → CTA with background image (heavy) → Footer (light).

### Scale contrast
- When a page has multiple images, they should NOT all be the same size. Create scale contrast: one large hero image, medium product screenshots, small avatar thumbnails. The variety in scale creates visual interest.

### Breaking the grid
- While content should follow the grid, images can occasionally break it — a product mockup that bleeds into the next section, a photo that extends beyond the content container width, an illustration that overlaps the section boundary. These "grid breaks" create energy and prevent monotony. Use one per page maximum.

## Step 6: UUI-Specific Image Patterns

These UUI components and sections are specifically designed to work with images — USE them:

### Hero header sections
- UUI hero variants support background images, split layouts with images, and centered layouts with images behind content. Never use a hero without exploring the image variant.

### Blog post cards
- Always fill the thumbnail area with a relevant image. The component expects it.

### Team member components
- Always fill with a portrait photo. Never leave the avatar as a placeholder gray circle.

### Portfolio mockup components
- UUI has device mockup frames (Macbook, phone, browser). Place product screenshots inside these — they instantly add professionalism.

### Background pattern components
- Multiple pattern options (geometric, dotted, lined, decorative). Place them behind sections at low opacity via blend mode adjustments.

### Social proof / Logo sections
- Designed for company logos. Fill with recognizable brand logos for credibility.

### Card components
- Many card variants include image placeholders. Fill them.

### Featured icon components
- These add colored-background circles/squares behind icons, making icon-based feature sections feel more visual and less like a text outline.

## Step 7: Image Generation Prompts — Be Specific

When generating images for a design, vague prompts produce generic stock-photo results. Be specific:

### Bad prompts
- "A photo of people working" → Generic, could be any company
- "Technology background" → Abstract meaningless blue gradient
- "Happy customer" → Stock photo smile

### Good prompts
Structure: [Subject] + [Action/Context] + [Environment] + [Lighting/Mood] + [Style]

- "A diverse team of software engineers collaborating around a large screen showing data visualizations, in a modern open-plan office with warm natural lighting, shot from a slightly elevated angle, editorial photography style"
- "Close-up of hands typing on a laptop keyboard with a dashboard interface visible on screen, shallow depth of field, warm office lighting, professional product photography"
- "Abstract geometric pattern of overlapping translucent circles in muted purple and gray tones, suitable as a subtle background texture, minimal and clean"

### Match the register
- **Minimalist register:** Clean, airy photography with lots of negative space. Muted colors. Single subjects.
- **Editorial/Luxury register:** Rich, contrasty photography. Dramatic lighting. Cinematic compositions.
- **Bold/Expressive register:** Saturated colors. Dynamic angles. Energetic subjects.
- **Professional/Corporate register:** Clean, well-lit, balanced compositions. Neutral environments. Polished but not flashy.

## Step 8: Anti-Patterns — Things That Make Images Look Bad

### ❌ Stretched or distorted images
Always use FILL or FIT scale mode. Never manually stretch an image to fit a container if the aspect ratio doesn't match.

### ❌ Low-contrast text over busy images
If the image has complex detail everywhere, text becomes unreadable. Use an overlay or reposition text to a calmer area of the image.

### ❌ Inconsistent image styles in a grid
Cards in a row where one has an illustration, one has a photo, and one has a screenshot. All images in a grid must share the same visual category.

### ❌ Placeholder gray boxes left in the final design
If a component has an image slot, FILL IT. Gray placeholder rectangles scream "unfinished."

### ❌ Images with no clear subject over text
A busy image used as a text background where the viewer can't focus on either the image content or the text. Choose images with clear areas of calm for text placement, or use a stronger overlay.

### ❌ Overusing the same image treatment everywhere
Every section has a full-bleed photo with dark overlay and white text. Variety matters — mix full-bleed images with contained images, overlaid images with adjacent images.

### ❌ Ignoring dark mode image behavior
Images that look great on white backgrounds can look jarring on dark backgrounds. Consider: reducing image brightness in dark mode, adding a subtle border in dark mode, or using images with darker tonality for dark themes.

### ❌ Using images as decoration instead of communication
Every image should serve a PURPOSE — showing the product, showing the people, setting the mood, establishing credibility. If an image doesn't communicate anything specific, it's visual noise. A well-chosen icon might be better than a meaningless stock photo.

## Decision Checklist Before Finalizing Any Design

Before declaring a design complete, walk through this:

1. Does the hero have an image or meaningful visual element? If not, add one.
2. Are there more than 3 consecutive sections without any image? If yes, add visual elements.
3. Do all cards with image slots have images filled? If not, generate or place them.
4. Do all testimonials have avatar photos? If not, add them.
5. Is text readable over every image? If not, add overlays or adjust placement.
6. Are all images in grids the same aspect ratio? If not, standardize them.
7. Is there visual rhythm — alternating heavy and light sections? If not, reorganize.
8. Have you used any UUI background patterns or visual components? If not, consider adding them for depth.
9. Would a non-designer look at this and see a finished product — or a wireframe? Be honest.
